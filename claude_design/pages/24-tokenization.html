Before the model sees text, it sees integers.

A tokenizer chops a stream of UTF-8 bytes into chunks the model knows about — usually 50K to 200K of them. Get this layer wrong and your model can't spell, can't count, can't multiply, and bills you twice as many tokens for the privilege.

Tokenizers are the most-ignored, most-blamed component of every LLM stack. They decide your context limit, your bill, and whether your model can do arithmetic.

Why not just characters or words? the goldilocks problem

Two obvious extremes both fail:

  • Character-level. Vocab is tiny (256 bytes), but sequences are 4× longer. Compute scales as $O(N^2)$, so this is a disaster for long context.
  • Word-level. Sequences are short, but you need a 500K+ vocab to cover real text — and you still get <UNK> on every typo, name, or new word.

Subword tokenization splits the difference. Common words become single tokens; rare or novel words decompose into reusable pieces. The dominant algorithm is Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), used by GPT-2/3/4, Llama, Claude, and most production models.

BPE in one paragraph greedy merge

Start with a vocab of single bytes (or characters). Count every adjacent pair in your training corpus. Find the most frequent pair — say (t, h) — and add th to the vocab as a new symbol. Re-tokenize. Repeat 50,000 times. The vocab now contains common letters, common digrams, common syllables, common words, and common phrases — graded by frequency.

The tax on different scripts not all languages cost the same

BPE is trained on a corpus — usually mostly English. So English compresses well: ~4 characters per token. Other scripts pay a tokenizer tax because their characters never made it into common merges. Rough numbers for the same 100-character message:

~30

~62

~73

// real-world consequence
Same prompt, different language: a Burmese user pays roughly 3× the per-character cost of an English user, hits context limits faster, and waits longer for first token. Newer tokenizers (GPT-4o, Gemini) deliberately broaden their training mix to compress non-English better.

Why your model can't count letters tokens hide the alphabet

Ask a model "how many R's are in strawberry?" and it will often say two. Why? Because strawberry tokenizes as something like [straw][berry] — the model never sees individual letters. Counting characters inside a token is a parlor trick we never trained it to do.

Same goes for: reversing strings, splitting CamelCase, doing digit-by-digit arithmetic. All of these get easier when the tokenizer is tuned to split digits individually and preserve byte-level fallbacks (which GPT-4o does, but earlier GPTs did not).

BPE vs SentencePiece vs WordPiece the algorithm zoo

  • BPE (byte-level). GPT-2, GPT-4, Llama, Mistral. Operates on UTF-8 bytes — never produces <UNK>.
  • SentencePiece (unigram). T5, mT5, ALBERT. Probabilistic; supports sampling alternative tokenizations during training (subword regularization).
  • WordPiece. BERT, DistilBERT. Like BPE but picks merges that maximize likelihood, not frequency.
  • Tiktoken. OpenAI's super-fast Rust BPE; uses regex pre-tokenization to keep merges sane (numbers, contractions, punctuation).

Practical takeaways

  • Estimate ~4 chars per token for English when planning context budgets — and test before shipping multilingual.
  • Pre-tokenize numbers digit-by-digit if your task involves arithmetic. Force splits with 1 2 3 4 instead of 1234.
  • Special tokens are cheap. <|im_start|>, <|tool_call|> etc. are added to the vocab as single tokens — they cost 1 each.
  • Tokenizer mismatch is silent. Embed with one tokenizer, generate with another → garbled output. Always verify the tokenizer ID in your model card.
